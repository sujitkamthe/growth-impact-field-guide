## Overview

Everyone has one scope, which describes how far their impact is expected to reach. The scope sets what is expected of you in three areas of work, and you grow by taking on some of the next scope's responsibilities until they become consistent.

## Scopes

- **Direct**: your own work
- **Extended**: work beyond your own
- **Team**: the whole team
- **Wider**: beyond the team

A wider scope still includes the work of the ones inside it.

## Areas

- **Client & Delivery**: building the right thing, building it well and delivering it reliably.
- **People & Team**: helping the people around you become more effective.
- **Org & Community**: contributing to Sahaj beyond your immediate delivery. You choose how, but contributing is part of the job.

Client & Delivery and People & Team each have three [facets](#area/client-delivery), and all three count toward the area's one rating. The lines under a facet show what it looks like at your scope; they are signs to look for rather than a checklist. Org & Community has [routes](#area/org-community) instead: expectations shared by everyone at your scope, and routes you choose from to meet them.

<!-- callout -->
A clear gap in any facet makes the area below expectations, however strong the other facets are. A gap means the facet's impact isn't shown consistently at your scope, not that one line is missing.

Learning runs through all three. At every scope you keep your craft and judgment current, act on feedback, and adapt when the context changes.

## Your reference scope

Your reference scope is the scope you have shown consistently across all three areas. You choose it when you write your expectations. If it doesn't match the impact your reviewers have seen, it is corrected during the review, before the doc is agreed. It sets the same expectations in every area. If you're new to Sahaj, it is agreed from the impact your experience shows, and nobody expects you to meet every expectation in your first few weeks, while you learn what's new to you.

You will be stronger in some areas than others, and the rating captures that: below, meets or exceeds expectations in each area. Exceeds means impact clearly stronger, broader or more durable than your scope expects.

<!-- callout -->
Exceeds is judged against your current scope, and it doesn't change your scope: that happens only when your next-scope impact is consistent in all three areas. Being strong in one area doesn't make up for falling short in another.

## Growing into the next scope

- **Meet your scope**: consistently, in all three areas
- **Take on more**: some of the next scope's responsibilities, usually as a stretch
- **Others rely on it**: next-scope impact becomes consistent in all three areas
- **Your scope is raised**: at the team check

A stretch adds some of the next scope's responsibilities to your current expectations rather than replacing them. It usually starts in one area, and consistent next-scope impact there is rated as exceeding. Your scope is raised once that impact holds in all three areas, because your reference scope is the one you show everywhere. It doesn't need every next-scope expectation met perfectly, only a pattern others rely on rather than a single strong instance, and there is no fixed number of cycles.

A stretch is the usual way to grow, but you don't need one on record to be raised: impact that is already consistent at the next scope counts however it started. Two situations call for one in particular. If you are growing quickly, you can take on next-scope responsibilities as soon as you're ready, in one area or more. If you have been at the same scope for a long time, or your scope is below what your experience suggests, your reviewers set a stretch with you in all three areas, so that growth is planned rather than left to chance.

## The cycle

- **Write your expectations**: choose your scope and any stretch, and draft your doc
- **Get it reviewed**: teammates ask for changes until it's agreed
- **Gather feedback**: ask for it often, and keep notes
- **Self-assess**: write it in the doc, then discuss it at the team check

You drive your own doc, a short one made from [the template](doc-templates/expectations.docx): your scope, anything specific to your work, where you want to grow, and later your self-assessment. The expectations themselves stay in this guide, so the doc only notes your scope. Upload it to Google Drive to work on it as a Google Doc and share it with your reviewers. [Reviewing and feedback](#teammates) is the guide for the people who review it. Through the cycle you ask the people you work with for feedback, often and while the work is fresh, and keep notes of what they tell you, wherever you keep notes. That way your [self-assessment](#self-assessment) starts from evidence already written down, rather than from what you remember at the end. The cycle ends with the team check, a conversation in which teammates from your project and office confirm, correct or add to your self-assessment; you can present the doc there, or let others read it beforehand for context. A raise in scope is decided there too, and the next cycle's doc is written at the new scope.

## Who reviews and decides

- **Everyone else on a team**: the team's seniors review their expectations, and confirm their ratings at the team check
- **Seniors**: their account lead, or seniors from other teams, review their expectations and confirm their ratings
- **Account leads**: seniors or leads from other accounts in the office review their expectations and confirm their ratings

Anyone on the team can also review someone's expectations, discuss them and ask for changes. If you and your reviewers still disagree after discussing it, about your scope or a rating, the account lead decides, or, when it is an account lead's own doc, a founder or the region's managing director.

## Principles

<!-- band -->
- **Expectations are written down first.** If they were never made explicit, that is the first gap to fix.
- **Evidence is a pattern.** One good example shows you can; consistency, and others relying on it, shows you do.
- **A well-made attempt counts.** If you take on a real problem and work it well, it counts even when factors outside your control stop it from succeeding. Working it well means staying with it: trying more than one approach, raising what's in the way early, bringing people along and adapting. Judge the problem you took on, how you went about it and what it left behind, such as groundwork, learning or a clear decision to stop. Giving up at the first setback, or an attempt without real effort behind it, is not impact, and neither is not taking the problem on.
- **Roles are routes to impact.** No scope requires a particular role, and leading a team or an account doesn't set your scope.
- **Growth means wider trust.** Doing more of the same work, or doing it better, is not the next scope.
- **You can always go beyond your scope.** Your scope sets what's expected, never what you're allowed to do.
- **Every office uses the same guide.** "Team scope" means the same thing in Pune as in London.
