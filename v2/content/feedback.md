## Why it matters

*Feedback is how the expectations agreed at the start of the cycle stay in view until the team check.*

When nobody says during the cycle that something is expected, it comes up for the first time in the self-assessment, when it's too late to change anything. Feedback given when the moment happens prevents that, and giving it, and asking for it, is expected of everyone, whatever their scope. [Watch the film](#tour/feedback) for the short version.

## What useful feedback says

<!-- group: Giving it | Soon, specific, about the work | feedback -->
*Say what you saw and what it changed, soon after the moment, and talk about the work, not the person.*

- **What you saw**: the specific moment, so they can recall it.
- **What it changed**: for the team, the client or the work.
- **What you'd suggest**: when something could go better, what to try instead.

<!-- more -->
If it helps them, say which area or facet it relates to, such as "that's consulting", so they can find it again at their self-assessment; it doesn't need to sound like a form. Give praise where others can hear it if they're comfortable with that, and anything critical in private. When someone asks you for feedback, answer soon and specifically.

## Cover all three areas

*Feedback on the work someone delivered covers one area; over the cycle, give feedback on all three.*

Most feedback is about the latest piece of work, because that is what people see every day. It matters, but on its own it covers only part of Client & Delivery and leaves out most of what is expected of the person. Over the cycle, make sure your feedback reaches all three areas:

- **Client & Delivery**: how they built the work, how they framed the problem with the client, and how they owned its commitments: whether they questioned a date they didn't believe in, kept the client's expectations realistic and went back to the client when something changed.
- **People & Team**: who grew because of them, how they gave and acted on feedback, and how they kept the people around them informed.
- **Org & Community**: what they contributed beyond the project, and what it changed.

## Recognise, correct and ask for more

*Good feedback says what went well, what should change, and where someone could take on more.*

- **Recognise what went well**: name the moment and what it changed, so they know to keep doing it.
- **Say what should change**: soon and in private, with what you'd suggest trying instead. That includes what the team should change for them, such as an opportunity, support or time they're missing.
- **Ask for more**: point to something they could take on next, such as evaluating a second approach before choosing one, or writing up what the team learned so other teams can use it. Tie it to a need, and say what time or support would make it possible, so it doesn't become one more thing to fit into their evenings.

## Asking for it, and hearing it

<!-- group: Asking for it | Ask small and often, and show what you changed | check -->
*Ask a small, specific question often, listen without defending, and show what you changed.*

- **Ask small and specific**: "What's one thing I could do differently in the next demo?" gets a usable answer, where "any feedback?" usually gets "all good".
- **Listen before you explain**: if it stings or seems wrong, pause and ask a question, such as "What did you see?" or "What would you have done?", before deciding what you think of it.
- **Thank them, and close the loop**: say what you'll try, and later show what changed. If you decide not to act on something, say why, so they keep telling you.

<!-- more -->
The most experienced people on a team set the pattern by asking first, including asking the newest teammates. Feedback goes in every direction, whatever anyone's scope, and how the team receives its first critical note decides whether the next one is given at all.

## The people already doing well

*People who are already strong need feedback and a next challenge too, or they stop growing.*

It is easy to give strong performers less feedback, because nothing seems to need fixing. Without it they stop being stretched, and after a while they stop growing and lose interest in their work. Tell them specifically what their work changed, and talk with them about what would challenge them next: a responsibility they haven't had before, a harder problem, or a stretch into the next scope. Recognition and a next challenge go together, because recognition on its own doesn't keep someone growing.

People who are strong in every area also tend to pick up whatever falls between other people's work. That is impact of their own, so name it in your feedback, and don't let it become their responsibility by default.

## When someone says no

*When someone turns down one way to contribute, ask what they will do instead.*

People differ in what they enjoy doing, and the guide leaves room for that: someone who doesn't enjoy writing doesn't have to write. What they can't do is leave the area empty. When someone declines a way to contribute, accept the choice, ask what they will do instead and agree it, so the conversation ends with a contribution rather than a gap. If what they want is to take less of a whole responsibility rather than skip one activity, that needs an [agreed focus](#specialisation), which takes more than a preference.

## Looking ahead at each team check

<!-- group: At the team check | Look ahead while there's a cycle to act | team -->
*Feedback in the moment rarely notices what's missing, so each team check also looks ahead to the next cycle.*

Feedback in the moment covers what people see. It rarely covers what's missing, because nobody has a reason to comment on work someone hasn't done. So after confirming the cycle that has ended, the team check looks ahead, and every cycle's check counts: an expectation that matters comes up in the cycle it applies to, not saved for a later one.

1. **What has each teammate seen?**: in each area, the contribution they've noticed.
2. **Where is evidence missing?**: an area, a facet or an agreed stretch that nobody has seen yet.
3. **What changes next cycle?**: different opportunities, support or time, and who will arrange them.
4. **Record what changed**: write any changed expectation in the next cycle's doc, so the next self-assessment measures against it.

<!-- more -->
Look at any agreed focus or Org & Community deferral here too: whether the greater contribution is happening, and whether the arrangement is still sustainable for the teammate who took on the handed-over responsibility.

## Examples

<!-- tips: Examples -->
*Feedback when something went well, when something could go better, on delivery ownership, asking for more, and for someone already doing well.*

> **When something went well**
>
> In Tuesday's design review you laid out two options for the refund flow with their costs, and the client decided in the meeting rather than a week later. Client & Delivery, consulting.

> **When something could go better**
>
> When our new joiner asked you about the flaky checkout test on Monday, you fixed it yourself in ten minutes. It unblocked them, but they still don't know why it failed, and they were stuck on the same test again on Wednesday. Next time, could you pair with them on it, even if it takes longer? People & Team, growing others.

> **Delivery ownership**
>
> In Thursday's planning, the client asked for the reporting feature by the end of the month and you agreed straight away, although the estimate we had just made was six weeks. Now we either work long hours or miss the date. Next time, could you show them the estimate and the two options we have, a smaller first release or a later date, and let them choose? Client & Delivery, delivery ownership.

> **Asking for more**
>
> Your fix for the slow search queries halved page load times, and the client noticed it in the showcase. Client & Delivery, technical craft. Two other teams have the same problem: would you write up what you tried and what worked, or run a short session for them? I'll move the logging work to next sprint to make the time. Org & Community, community and external contribution.

> **For someone already doing well**
>
> You've run the client's planning sessions well all cycle, and their trust in our estimates shows it. People & Team, communication and collaboration. The client wants to review the whole platform's roadmap next quarter: would you lead that with me, and take it over after the first session? Client & Delivery, consulting.
