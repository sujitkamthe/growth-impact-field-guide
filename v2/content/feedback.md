## Why it matters

Feedback is for your growth: a teammate's view of what you could try next, while there's still time to try it, not a mark for your self-assessment. So when you notice something, say it, rather than holding it back to be kind or saving it for the team check, and when it's said to you, hear it for what you could do differently. Giving it and asking for it are expected of everyone, whatever their scope. [Watch the film](#tour/feedback) for the short version.

## How often

*Small and often: in the moment, a question every week, and a look ahead at each team check.*

- **In the moment**: the same day, while you both remember it, for praise and for small corrections alike.
- **Every week**: ask at least one person one small question, such as after a demo, a review or a client meeting.
- **At each team check**: look ahead at what nobody has seen yet. It's where feedback is reviewed, not where it starts.

<!-- more: Why not monthly, or before the self-assessment -->
Collecting feedback once a month, or in the weeks before your self-assessment, turns it into a batch of verdicts that arrive too late to act on, and makes it feel like evidence for a rating. If the first feedback you ask for in a cycle comes the week before your self-assessment, it's too late to change anything that cycle.


## Giving it

<!-- group: Giving it | Soon after the moment, specific, about the work | feedback -->
*Say what you saw and what it changed, soon after the moment, and talk about the work, not the person.*

<!-- note -->
> **What you saw** When our new joiner asked you about the flaky checkout test on Monday, you fixed it yourself in ten minutes.
>
> **What it changed** It unblocked them, but they still don't know why it failed, and they were stuck on the same test again on Wednesday.
>
> **What you'd suggest** Next time, could you pair with them on it, even if it takes longer?

<!-- more: When something went well, and naming the area -->
When something went well, say the first two and skip the suggestion: "In Tuesday's design review you laid out two options for the refund flow with their costs, and the client decided in the meeting rather than a week later." If it helps, name the area or facet, such as "that's consulting", so they know which area to keep growing in; it doesn't need to sound like a form. Give praise where others can hear it if they're comfortable with that, and anything critical in private. Say it to the person it's about, not to someone else about them, and don't save it for an anonymous form: they can only ask what you meant, or tell you what you missed, if they know it came from you.

## Anyone to anyone, without fear

*Say what you see to anyone, whatever their scope or how long they've been here.*

Sahaj has no titles and open salaries, and saying what you see, to anyone, is the everyday part of that same openness. How long someone has been here, how much they know, or how close they are to the client is never a reason to hold feedback back, in either direction: the newest person on a team often sees what everyone else has stopped noticing.

<!-- more: What makes it safe -->
It stays safe because of how people respond. If you've been on the team the longest, ask for feedback about your own work, and thank the person who tells you something critical, even if you disagree. If you react badly, they won't tell you again, and nor will anyone who saw it. A critical note never counts against the person who gave it.

## Cover all three areas

*Feedback on the work someone delivered covers one area; over the cycle, give feedback on all three.*

- **Client & Delivery**: how they built it, how they framed the problem with the client, and whether they kept its commitments realistic.
- **People & Team**: who grew because of them, how they gave and acted on feedback, and how they kept people informed.
- **Org & Community**: what they contributed beyond the project, and what it changed.

<!-- more: See an example about delivery ownership -->
> In Thursday's planning, the client asked for the reporting feature by the end of the month and you agreed straight away, although the estimate we had just made was six weeks. Now we either work long hours or miss the date. Next time, could you show them the estimate and the two options we have, a smaller first release or a later date, and let them choose?

## Recognise, correct and ask for more

*Good feedback says what went well, what should change, and where someone could take on more.*

- **Recognise what went well**: name the moment and what it changed, so they know to keep doing it.
- **Say what should change**: soon and in private, with something to try instead, including what the team should change for them, such as an opportunity, support or time they're missing.
- **Ask for more**: point to something they could take on next, tie it to a need, and say what time or support makes it possible.

<!-- more: See an example that asks for more -->
> Your fix for the slow search queries halved page load times, and the client noticed it in the showcase. Two other teams have the same problem: would you write up what you tried, or run a short session for them? I'll move the logging work to next sprint to make the time.

## Asking for it

<!-- group: Asking for it | Ask small and often, and show what you changed | check -->
*Ask a small, specific question often, listen without defending, and show what you changed.*

Hear feedback as being for your growth, not as a judgement of you: it's about something you did, not who you are, and you can hear it and still be good at what you do.

- **Ask small and specific**: "What's one thing I could do differently in the next demo?" gets a usable answer, where "any feedback?" usually gets "all good".
- **Listen before you explain**: if it stings, or you feel a "yes, but" coming, pause and ask "What did you see?" or "What would you have done?" before deciding what you think of it.
- **Hear the what before the who**: if you notice yourself dismissing it because of who said it, ask what they saw and whether any of it is true. How it was said can be its own conversation.
- **Thank them, and close the loop**: say what you'll try, and later show what changed. If you decide not to act on something, say why, so they keep telling you.

<!-- more: Who to ask -->
Ask the teammate most likely to see it differently, not only the one who'll be kind. The more experienced you get, the more useful it is to hear what isn't working yet. If people get a defence when they tell you something critical, they stop telling you.

## The people already doing well

*People who are already strong need feedback and a next challenge too, or they stop growing.*

Tell them specifically what their work changed, and talk with them about what would challenge them next: a responsibility they haven't had before, a harder problem, or a stretch into the next scope. People who are strong in every area also tend to pick up whatever falls between other people's work; that is impact of their own, so name it, and don't let it become their responsibility by default.

<!-- more: See an example for someone doing well -->
> You've run the client's planning sessions well all cycle, and their trust in our estimates shows it. The client wants to review the whole platform's roadmap next quarter: would you lead that with me, and take it over after the first session?

## When someone says no

*When someone turns down one way to contribute, ask what they will do instead.*

<!-- more: Agreeing what they'll do instead -->
Someone who doesn't enjoy writing doesn't have to write, but they can't leave the area empty. Accept the choice, ask what they'll do instead and agree it, so the conversation ends with a contribution rather than a gap. Taking less of a whole responsibility, rather than skipping one activity, needs an [agreed focus](#specialisation).

## Looking ahead at each team check

<!-- group: At the team check | Look ahead while there's a cycle to act | team -->
*Feedback in the moment rarely notices what's missing, so each team check also looks ahead to the next cycle.*

<!-- row -->
1. **What has each teammate seen?**: in each area, the contribution they've noticed.
2. **Where is evidence missing?**: an area, a facet or an agreed stretch nobody has seen yet.
3. **What changes next cycle?**: opportunities, support or time, and who arranges them.
4. **Record what changed**: in the next cycle's doc, so the next self-assessment measures against it.

<!-- more: Agreed focus and deferrals at the team check -->
Every cycle's check counts: an expectation that matters comes up in the cycle it applies to, not saved for a later one. Look at any agreed focus or Org & Community deferral here too: whether the greater contribution is happening, and whether the arrangement is still sustainable for the teammate who took on the work.
