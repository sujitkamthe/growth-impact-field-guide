## Why it matters

A piece of feedback is a teammate's view of what you could try next, while there's still time to try it: an observation to explore, not a rating. Your self-assessment later looks at patterns across the cycle, not at single comments. So when you notice something, say it, rather than holding it back to be kind or saving it for the team check, and when it's said to you, hear it for what you could do differently. Giving it and asking for it are expected of everyone, whatever their scope. [Watch the film](#tour/feedback) for the short version.

## How often

*Small and often, around the work you already do.*

- **Soon after the moment**: after a demo, a review, a pairing session or a client conversation, while you both remember it. A hard conversation can wait until you can talk in private.
- **Every week**: give one teammate an observation and ask one a small question. If you've been on the team the longest, start the conversation, including with the newest people.
- **In a one-to-one now and then**: a longer conversation with a teammate about patterns, whose view is missing, and whether earlier feedback helped.

<!-- more: Why not save it up -->
Saving observations for the end of the month, or for the weeks before your self-assessment, turns them into a batch of verdicts that arrive too late to act on. A monthly look at patterns can add to feedback given as work happens, but it can't replace it. There's no count of feedback to reach and no weekly note to file: the habit is the point. Someone who never gives or asks for feedback hears about it during the cycle, because giving it is part of People & Team.

## Giving it

<!-- group: Give feedback | Soon after the moment, specific, about the work | feedback -->
*Say what you saw and what it changed, soon after the moment, and talk about the work, not the person.*

<!-- note -->
> **What you saw** When our new joiner asked you about the flaky checkout test on Monday, you fixed it yourself in ten minutes.
>
> **What it changed** It unblocked them, but they still don't know why it failed, and they were stuck on the same test again on Wednesday.
>
> **What you'd suggest** Next time, could you pair with them on it, even if it takes longer?
>
> **How did you see it?** Then ask, because you may have missed something they knew.

<!-- more: When it went well, naming the area, and who to say it to -->
When something went well, say the first two and skip the suggestion: "In Tuesday's design review you laid out two options for the refund flow with their costs, and the client decided in the meeting rather than a week later." You don't need the answer to raise a problem: "this made it hard for me to join in" is useful even if you don't know the fix. If it helps, name the area or facet, such as "that's consulting", so they know which area to keep growing in. Give praise where others can hear it if they're comfortable with that, and anything critical in private. Say it to the person it's about, not to someone else about them, wherever that feels safe; if it doesn't, see When it feels difficult.

## Anyone to anyone, without fear

*Say what you see to anyone, whatever their scope or how long they've been here.*

<!-- more: Why, and what keeps it safe -->
Sahaj has no titles and open salaries, and saying what you see, to anyone, is the everyday part of that same openness. How long someone has been here, how much they know, or how close they are to the client is never a reason to hold feedback back, in either direction: the newest person on a team often sees what everyone else has stopped noticing.

No titles doesn't mean no difference in influence: someone may still arrange client access, allocate work or take part in your team check. It stays safe because of how people respond. If you've been on the team the longest, ask for feedback about your own work, thank the person who tells you something critical even if you disagree, and share what you changed. If you react badly, they won't tell you again, and nor will anyone who saw it.

## Cover all three areas

*Feedback on the work someone delivered covers one area; over the cycle, give feedback on all three.*

- **Client & Delivery**: how they built it, how they framed the problem with the client, and whether they kept its commitments realistic.
- **People & Team**: who grew because of them, how they gave and acted on feedback, and how they kept people informed.
- **Building Sahaj**: what they contributed beyond the project, and what it changed.

<!-- more: See an example about delivery ownership -->
> In Thursday's planning, the client asked for the reporting feature by the end of the month and you agreed straight away, although the estimate we had just made was six weeks. Now we either work long hours or miss the date. Next time, could you show them the estimate and the two options we have, a smaller first release or a later date, and let them choose?

## Recognise, correct and ask for more

*Good feedback says what went well, what should change, and where someone could take on more.*

- **Recognise what went well**: name the moment and what it changed, so they know to keep doing it.
- **Say what should change**: soon and in private, with one thing to try, including what the team should change for them, such as an opportunity, support or time they're missing.
- **Ask for more**: point to something they could take on next, tie it to a need, and say what time or support makes it possible.

<!-- more: See examples: asking for more, and for someone doing well -->
> Your fix for the slow search queries halved page load times, and the client noticed it in the showcase. Two other teams have the same problem: would you write up what you tried, or run a short session for them? I'll move the logging work to next sprint to make the time.

People already doing well need feedback and a next challenge too, or they stop growing. Tell them what their work changed and ask what they'd like to take on next; if they pick up whatever falls between other people's work, name that as impact of their own.

> You've run the client's planning sessions well all cycle, and their trust in our estimates shows it. The client wants to review the whole platform's roadmap next quarter: would you lead that with me, and take it over after the first session?

## When someone says no

*When someone turns down one way to contribute, ask what they will do instead.*

<!-- more: Agreeing what they'll do instead -->
Someone who doesn't enjoy writing doesn't have to write, but they can't leave the area empty. Accept the choice, ask what they'll do instead and agree it, so the conversation ends with a contribution rather than a gap. Taking less of a whole responsibility, rather than skipping one activity, needs an [agreed focus](#specialisation).

## Ask and respond

<!-- group: Ask and respond | Ask small and often, listen, and show what you changed | check -->
*Ask a small, specific question often, listen before you explain, and show what you changed.*

It's about something you did, not who you are, and you can hear it and still be good at what you do.

- **Ask about what you're working on**: "I'm trying to make client trade-offs clearer. What did you notice in that conversation?" gets a usable answer, where "any feedback?" usually gets "all good".
- **Listen, then explain**: if it stings, or you feel a "yes, but" coming, ask "What did you see?" or "What would you have done?" first. Then add any context they didn't have, and agree what to try or say why you see it differently.
- **Hear the what before the who**: if you notice yourself dismissing it because of who said it, ask what they saw and whether any of it is true. How it was said can be its own conversation.
- **Close the loop**: thank them, try one change, and later go back: "I tried your suggestion; here's what happened." If you decide not to act on something, say why, so they keep telling you.

<!-- more: Who to ask, and disagreeing -->
Ask the teammate most likely to see it differently, not only the one who'll be kind. The more experienced you get, the more useful it is to hear what isn't working yet. Disagreeing with a piece of feedback doesn't mean you're resisting growth; explaining what they couldn't see is part of the conversation.

## Get a wider view

<!-- group: Get a wider view | Hear from beyond your usual circle | areas -->
*Over the cycle, ask people who see different parts of your work, not only the people you sit with.*

- **Someone you work alongside**: how you collaborate, your technical decisions and how you own your commitments.
- **Someone who relies on your guidance or reviews**: whether they're building judgment of their own because of you.
- **A client or colleague affected by your commitments**: how you consult, communicate and manage expectations.
- **Someone you worked with on building Sahaj**: how reliable you are, and what changed beyond your project.

<!-- more: Three questions to ask them, and a more structured round -->
This is a spread of views, not a list to ask everyone; people should only comment on work they've seen, and can say they haven't seen enough. Three questions work well: What contribution of mine has helped you, and can you give an example? What could I change to make working with me more effective? Where could I contribute more, and what support would help? If your team runs a more structured round of feedback, agree its purpose and who will see the answers before anyone writes them.

## Looking ahead at each team check

*Each team check also looks ahead, because feedback in the moment rarely notices what's missing.*

<!-- row -->
1. **What has each teammate seen?**: in each area, the contribution they've noticed.
2. **Whose view is missing?**: an area, a facet or an agreed stretch nobody has seen yet.
3. **What changes next cycle?**: opportunities, support or time, and who arranges them.
4. **Record what changed**: in the next cycle's doc.

<!-- more: Agreed focus and deferrals at the team check -->
Don't wait for the team check to find that nobody has talked about someone's People & Team or Building Sahaj contribution: in a one-to-one during the cycle, ask whose view is missing. Look at any agreed focus or Building Sahaj deferral here too: whether the greater contribution is happening, and whether the arrangement is still sustainable for the teammate who took on the work.

## When it feels difficult

<!-- group: When it feels difficult | Pause, get help, and stay safe | disagree -->
*A hard conversation can be paused, helped or taken somewhere safe; nobody should face it alone.*

<!-- checks -->
- **You can pause it**: say you'd like to come back to it, and return with more context once you've both had time.
- **It stays private**: critical feedback stays between the people involved unless they agree otherwise.
- **It isn't quoted without agreement**: using feedback in a self-assessment doesn't mean naming or quoting the person who gave it.
- **Someone can help**: a trusted teammate can sit in on, or help prepare for, a difficult conversation.

<!-- more: If you fear it will count against you -->
A critical note should never count against the person who gave it. If you're worried that it will, or that it already has, talk to the seniors on your team; if your concern involves them, talk to your account lead, or a founder or the region's managing director, who are outside that team's influence over your team check.
